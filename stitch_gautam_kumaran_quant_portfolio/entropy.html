Entropy in the Stock Market

A study of whether Indian IT stocks enter unusually ordered periods together, and how they return to normal afterward.

View source on GitHub → Jump to conclusions
In plain terms

What this project is actually asking

Every day, a stock either goes up or goes down. Most of the time that's basically a coin flip: hard to predict, and each stock does its own thing. But sometimes a stock starts moving in one direction over and over, for weeks at a time. That's a more "ordered" stretch than usual: less random, more predictable.

This project asks two simple questions about those ordered stretches. First: when one stock enters one of these ordered periods, do other stocks in the same sector tend to enter one at the same time, or is it just a coincidence? Second: once a stock is in one of these ordered periods, how does it snap back to its normal, unpredictable self: quickly and predictably, or slowly and randomly?

The motivation came from physics: ideas like "order," "randomness," and "returning to equilibrium" show up all over statistical physics, and it turns out they map onto stock markets surprisingly well. So the project borrows entropy, a way of measuring how random or ordered something is, and applies it to 14 Indian IT stocks to see if those physics-style patterns actually show up in real market data.

Overview

Treating a stock market like a system moving away from equilibrium

Each stock's daily return is reduced to a single bit, up or down, and the "disorder" of that sequence is measured with Shannon entropy, the same quantity used in information theory and thermodynamics. Low entropy means a stock is moving with unusual directional persistence.

The problem

Do stocks enter low-entropy states independently, or do several enter them together? And once a stock is in that state, is its recovery predictable, or mostly stochastic?

What was built

A five-stage Python pipeline covering data collection, cleaning, entropy calculation, a coordination test, and a recovery-time analysis. The full analysis was swept across 4 window sizes and 5 Z-thresholds for robustness.

Dataset

Daily closing prices for 14 Indian IT-sector stocks plus the NIFTY IT index, spanning roughly 2006–2026.

Methodology

From raw prices to a recovery time

Python, using pandas, NumPy, SciPy, yfinance, and Matplotlib. No databases or infra beyond local CSVs; the priority was reproducibility.

01

Binary symbolic representation

Log returns rt = log(Pt / Pt−1) are collapsed to a single bit: 1 if the stock rose, 0 if it didn't. Magnitude is discarded on purpose; the pipeline studies directional order, not volatility.

02

Rolling Shannon entropy

H = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p) is computed over a rolling window of W trading days, where p is the fraction of up-days in the window. A stock-specific threshold (that stock's mean entropy minus Z standard deviations) flags "low-entropy" days.

Baseline: W = 45 days, Z = 1.75
03

Coordination test: Poisson-binomial null model

At each timestep, S(t) counts how many stocks are simultaneously low-entropy. Under independence, each stock has its own empirically estimated probability qi of being low-entropy, so S(t) should follow a Poisson-binomial distribution. A significance threshold K is chosen where crossing it would be unlikely by chance, then compared against the real data.

04

Relaxation analysis

For every low-entropy episode, two recovery times are tracked: time to cross back above the threshold, and time to fully return to the stock's mean entropy. These recovery times are then correlated against entropy depth, price movement, coordination level, and NIFTY IT sector entropy at entry.

Results

Coordinated onset, two-stage recovery

The numbers below are the study's headline findings.

Low-entropy states are coordinated, not independent

At baseline parameters (W=45, Z=1.75), the independence-based null model sets a significance threshold of K = 4 simultaneous stocks. The real data crossed that threshold 111 times, far more often than the null model predicts.

This held up across the full sweep of 4 window sizes and 5 Z-thresholds, so it isn't an artifact of one parameter choice.

Recovery happens on two different timescales

Stocks cross back above the low-entropy threshold quickly, with a mean of 6.2 days (median 3.0 days). Fully returning to typical entropy takes much longer, with a mean of 25.7 days (median 21.0 days).

Both distributions are right-skewed, with a small number of events taking much longer than average.

Entropy depth predicts the fast phase, not the slow one

How far entropy dropped strongly predicts how fast a stock crosses back over the threshold: r ≈ 0.76 (p < 0.0001). It barely predicts the full return to normal: r ≈ 0.26 (p < 0.0001).

Price-based event strength showed no meaningful correlation with either recovery measure (r ≈ −0.12 and r ≈ −0.13). Coordination level at entry also showed no meaningful relationship (r ≈ −0.02 and r ≈ 0.06). Sector-wide entropy conditions had no significant effect either (p ≈ 0.55).

Conclusions

A hybrid structure: collective onset, mostly local recovery

Collective organization

Low-entropy states are a genuine collective effect: multiple stocks enter ordered states together far more often than independence would predict.

Two-regime relaxation

A fast, structured early exit from the threshold, followed by a slow, largely stochastic return to typical behavior.

Recovery is local

Neither sector-wide conditions nor coordination with other stocks meaningfully shapes an individual stock's recovery time; that's governed by stock-specific dynamics.
